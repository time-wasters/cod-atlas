## The Multiplayer Map in the Game

*Mayenne* is a multiplayer map in *Call of Duty 3*. The Call of Duty Wiki
places it in **Mayenne, France**, identifies its terrain as an **indoor castle
with a large garden/graveyard**, and explicitly lists **Mayenne Bridge** as its
campaign map. Call of Duty Maps likewise describes the multiplayer arena as a
**12th-century castle set in Mayenne, France**.

The playable layout is dominated by the castle rather than by the river bridge
that gives the campaign mission its title. The Wiki describes corridors, a
foyer, a patio, a wine cellar, an underground route, a graveyard/garden area
and a small stone bridge around the castle-side buildings. This makes the
multiplayer map a much more specific environment than the generic city name in
the old marker.

## Campaign Relationship

The relationship to the single-player mission *Mayenne Bridge* is unusually
useful for locating this multiplayer map. The multiplayer Wiki explicitly
names *Mayenne Bridge* as its campaign map, while the campaign page lists
*Mayenne* among the multiplayer maps associated with the mission.

More importantly, the campaign's playable route contains a distinct castle
sequence that closely overlaps the multiplayer layout. Nichols and the squad
approach a **large castle**, clear its hallways and **foyer**, fight through a
**courtyard**, descend into a **wine cellar**, and then emerge into a
**garden** before continuing down to the river. Those are the same major
environmental elements emphasized in the multiplayer description. The
single-player mission then continues well beyond the castle: the squad reaches
the river, crosses by boat, fights through more streets and buildings, and only
later reaches the bridge-demolition finale.

That distinction matters for the Atlas marker. The researched campaign level
has a strong historical reason to use **Pont Mac-Racken** as its real-world
anchor: the mission's date, U.S. 90th Infantry Division, threatened bridge and
fatal engineer action closely parallel the documented events at that bridge on
5 August 1944. For the multiplayer map, however, using Pont Mac-Racken would
anchor the marker to the **final objective of the larger campaign mission**
rather than to the **castle environment actually reused as the multiplayer
arena**.

## The Real Place & Differences

The strongest real-world castle anchor is the **Château de Mayenne** in the
city of Mayenne. The French Ministry of Culture identifies the protected
Château de Mayenne in Mayenne, Pays de la Loire, and records principal
construction phases in the **11th and 12th centuries** as well as later work.
The official Musée du Château de Mayenne describes an even older Carolingian
core, with the site subsequently transformed into a fortified castle. Its
educational material explains that the 13th-century phase added a circular
keep, a large lower court, enclosure walls and a fortified entrance, while the
museum today also maintains a medieval-inspired garden in the high courtyard.

This gives the real château a good thematic and geographic fit for a game map
described simply as a 12th-century castle in Mayenne. It also sits in the
historic river-side core of the same city as Pont Mac-Racken, so choosing the
castle does not break the campaign's broader Mayenne setting.

The match should **not** be treated as proof of a one-to-one reconstruction.
No developer or production source found in this research explicitly states
that Treyarch modeled *Mayenne* on the Château de Mayenne, and no reliable
source identifies the multiplayer graveyard, wine cellar, foyer, patio,
tunnels or small stone bridge as surveyed copies of specific surviving parts
of the real château. The game's architectural layout is therefore best treated
as a fictionalized castle environment associated with Mayenne, with the real
Château de Mayenne serving as the strongest identifiable landmark anchor.

## Historical Context

The real fighting in Mayenne on **5 August 1944** strongly supports the
campaign mission's broader setting. The 90th Infantry Division after-action
report states that Task Force Weaver reached Mayenne, found a bridge across the
Mayenne River still intact, and rushed to seize it before German forces could
destroy it. The bridge had been prepared for demolition but was captured
intact.

U.S. Army Engineer Museum material identifies Private James D. McRacken of
Company A, 315th Engineer Battalion, 90th Infantry Division, as the soldier who
ran to the threatened bridge, cut the main control wire and was killed there.
The French heritage inventory identifies the surviving/reconstructed crossing
as the present **Pont Mac Racken**, and local Mayenne heritage material also
records the bridge as having been saved by McRacken.

This historical evidence explains why Pont Mac-Racken is a very strong marker
for the **single-player Mayenne Bridge mission**. It does not establish the
Château de Mayenne as a documented focal point of the August 1944 fighting,
which is another reason not to claim that the multiplayer castle is a literal
battlefield reconstruction.

This is a genuine precision improvement over the old marker rather than extra

The campaign marker at **Pont Mac-Racken** was also considered. It lies only a
