## The Mission in the Game

“Aftermath” is a very short singleplayer epilogue to “Shock and Awe” (`cod4-shock-and-awe`). It takes place on Day 3 in 2011 and continues with U.S. Marine Sgt. Paul Jackson immediately after the nuclear detonation that destroys the unnamed Middle Eastern capital at the end of the preceding mission. The CH-46 Sea Knight carrying Jackson and the other Marines has crashed in the blast zone.

There is no conventional briefing, combat objective, or extended route. Jackson regains consciousness in the wreck, crawls outside, and moves only a short distance through the devastated urban area. The level shows the destroyed helicopter, dead or dying Marines, burning and collapsing buildings, and the nuclear cloud before Jackson dies from his injuries and radiation exposure. The opening satellite/news material identifies the event only as a nuclear detonation in the Middle East and describes the destroyed place as a capital; it does not name a real country or city.

Because “Aftermath” begins at the crash site created in “Shock and Awe,” the two levels are geographically inseparable in the game narrative. Any real-world marker for “Aftermath” therefore has to be justified primarily from the geographic evidence available in the preceding mission rather than from a new location named here.

## The Real Place & Differences

The best-supported real-world anchor is the **Susangerd area in Khuzestan Province, Iran**, but the match should be classified as a **composite or fictionalized setting**, not as a confirmed depiction of Susangerd. The current Call of Duty Wiki material for “Shock and Awe” notes that its tracking display places Jackson near the real city of Susangerd. Since “Aftermath” starts moments later at the same helicopter crash site, that clue is stronger for this level than assigning an unrelated point elsewhere in Iraq.

Susangerd is a real city in western Khuzestan, in the low-lying Karkheh River plain. Academic work on the Karkheh delta describes the surrounding area as a broad, very flat alluvial landscape extending around Susangerd toward Bostan and Hoveyzeh. That broad terrain is at least compatible with some of the low, arid river-plain imagery used in the campaign, but there is no evidence that the game reproduces Susangerd’s real street plan, buildings, or landmarks.

The largest mismatch is scale and identity: the game repeatedly treats the destroyed settlement as the unnamed regime’s capital, whereas Susangerd is a regional city rather than a national capital. The dense fictional city and its nuclear destruction therefore cannot be treated as a reconstruction of the real place.

A competing secondary source, Call of Duty Maps, labels “Aftermath” as taking place in Basrah/Basra, Iraq. That attribution is more specific than the game itself but is not corroborated by the current Wiki location record, which retains only “Middle East,” and it conflicts with the predecessor mission’s game-derived Susangerd-area tracking clue. Basra is therefore rejected as the weaker anchor here. The earlier `33, 44` Iraq marker was only a country fallback; the Susangerd-area point is a better fit because it is tied to evidence visible in the mission sequence rather than merely to a presumed country.

## The Real Mission & Differences

No matching real 2011 U.S. Marine mission, nuclear detonation, or destruction of a Middle Eastern capital is documented for this location. “Aftermath” is fictional.

The campaign nevertheless borrows recognizable elements from the 2003 Iraq War. Official U.S. Marine Corps histories document the 1st Force Reconnaissance Company participating in Operation Iraqi Freedom, including operations during the opening invasion of Iraq. Contemporary U.S. Air Force historical material also uses “shock and awe” for the large conventional strikes against Iraqi leadership and military targets during the opening phase of the 2003 war.

Those historical connections do not make the fictional event a recreation of Operation Iraqi Freedom. The real invasion occurred in Iraq in 2003, not western Iran in 2011; the real bombardment was conventional rather than a hostile nuclear detonation destroying a capital; and the documented movements of 1st Force Recon do not provide a historical counterpart to Jackson’s fictional crash in the Susangerd area. The real-war material is best understood as thematic and unit-level inspiration, while the mission’s place and event remain fictionalized.
