## The Mission in the Game

*Raiding the Fortress* is the fourth and final North Africa mission in *Call of
Duty: Finest Hour*. It continues directly from *A Desert Ride*
(`cod-fh-a-desert-ride`), with no new journey or location change. Private
Edward Carlyle and Sergeant Bob Starkey begin behind the wrecked jeep that
crashed into the Roman fortress at Ksar Tarsine on January 23, 1943. Carlyle
serves with the game's fictionalized Popski's Private Army (PPA).

Starkey gives Carlyle demolition charges and sends him along the right flank
while other soldiers attack elsewhere. Carlyle seals an entrance, crosses the
fortress, and reaches a tower where Jack DeHart and Allen Rowe are trapped.
DeHart says that Harold Snyder, the patrol's cartographer, was captured and is
being held underground.

After defending the courtyard from German troops and Stuka attacks, Carlyle
destroys a half-track blocking the descent. He and DeHart enter what the game
calls the fortress's cistern system, free Snyder, reunite with Starkey, and
escape through an underground passage. The campaign then changes protagonists
and theatres; this is the end of Carlyle's story.

## The Real Place & Differences

Although this level's own title card calls the setting an unnamed Roman
fortress in Tunisia, its uninterrupted opening resolves the ambiguity. The
preceding mission explicitly identifies the destination as **Ksar Tarsine**, a
spelling variant of Ksar Tarcine. The real ruin is the late Roman Centenarium
Tibubuci in present-day Kebili Governorate, on the eastern edge of the Grand
Erg Oriental.

An inscription dates the post to approximately AD 303–305. Archaeological
descriptions show that it was a small frontier installation: a roughly
0.32-hectare enclosure surrounding a central building about 15 by 15 metres.
It occupied a low rise above Wadi Hallouf and served the Limes Tripolitanus,
with broad views over the arid steppe and passing caravan routes.

The game's fortress is substantially enlarged. Its broad courtyard, multiple
combat areas, towers, two large entrance arches, internal underground rooms,
and escape tunnel do not match the documented compact plan. A real cistern did
exist, which is an intriguing point of resemblance, but it was constructed in
the wadi about 50 metres below the fort rather than as a prison-and-tunnel
complex beneath the enclosure. There is no evidence that the game's cistern
layout is an archaeological reconstruction or that the surviving ruin had the
depicted World War II installations.

## The Real Mission & Differences

The surrounding historical premise draws on real LRDG and PPA reconnaissance.
In January 1943, the two organizations explored the difficult desert approaches
south and west of the Mareth Line. Bob Yunnie's memoir describes a thirteen-
vehicle joint patrol crossing largely unknown country so that Eighth Army
armour could later bypass the Axis defences. Academic research identifies an
LRDG patrol under Nick Wilder with finding the passage known as Wilder's Gap,
while Peniakoff, Jean Caneri, and Ken Tinker continued reconnaissance farther
toward the line.

The game's cartographer is therefore thematically plausible: navigation, route
logging, and geographic intelligence were central to the real operation. The
specific capture and rescue are fictional, however. No reviewed source
identifies Harold Snyder, Jack DeHart, or the game's other soldiers as members
of the historical patrol, and none records an LRDG cartographer imprisoned at
Ksar Tarcine.

The real crisis happened at a concealed vehicle camp, not inside a Roman fort.
While three jeeps conducted the forward reconnaissance, German aircraft found
the PPA–LRDG camp in a wadi about 60 miles south of Matmata. Yunnie records nine
trucks and the wireless jeep being destroyed and two LRDG men being wounded.
The surviving patrol then faced a difficult withdrawal without the level's
fortress assault or prisoner extraction.

The reconnaissance nevertheless had an important result. In March 1943,
New Zealand Corps used the southern route around the Matmata Hills during the
outflanking move toward the Tebaga Gap. *Raiding the Fortress* combines that
real intelligence success with an invented German occupation, siege, rescue,
and underground escape. No evidence was found for a battle at Centenarium
Tibubuci on January 23.
