## The Mission in the Game

In *Call of Duty 2*, “The Brigade Box” is a singleplayer mission played as Sergeant John Davis of the British 7th Armoured Division. The loading journal dates it to 09:00 on 14 June 1944 at Amayé-sur-Seulles. It follows “The Tiger”: Davis says the division was mauled “yesterday” by a Tiger near Villers-Bocage and has been ordered to pull back toward Caumont in a defensive box, abandoning the immediate drive on Caen.

The playable route starts with a run across a mortar-swept field into farm buildings, then has Davis and his squad destroy mortar positions, seize a German field headquarters, defend it against infantry and a half-track, and finally disable a Tiger tank with explosives. Captain Price then says this flank action allowed the 7th Armoured Division to get out. This is the last mission of the British campaign. The repository’s next playable level is “The Battle of Pointe du Hoc”, which switches to the American campaign and returns to 6 June, so it does not continue Davis’s route.

The transition from the previous mission is historically compressed. “The Tiger” itself is dated 12 June at St. Louet, while this journal calls the Tiger ambush “yesterday” and relocates it to just east of Villers-Bocage. The latter wording resembles the real Villers-Bocage fighting of 13 June more closely than the previous mission’s literal date and place.

## The Real Place & Differences

Amayé-sur-Seulles is a real commune in Calvados, Normandy, west of Villers-Bocage on the road toward Caumont. The historical 22nd Armoured Brigade Group’s defensive position on 13–14 June was in this same area, so the level has a confirmed real geographical and historical basis rather than merely a loose analogue.

The exact footprint of the brigade box is less certain. The Queen’s Royal Hussars Museum places it on the high ground east of Amayé, north and south of the road through Saint-Germain. Published secondary histories differ over whether the best center is Hill 174, south of Amayé, or around Saint-Germain north of Tracy-Bocage. The real position was a dispersed, all-round bocage defensive perimeter, not a demonstrably identifiable field headquarters or fixed route matching the game. The game’s compact sequence of fields, farm buildings and one defensible HQ is therefore best treated as a fictionalized/compressed representation of a confirmed battlefield area.

## The Real Mission & Differences

Historically, the 7th Armoured Division’s thrust through the Caumont gap was part of Operation Perch, intended to turn the German flank via Villers-Bocage. On 13 June the leading brigade group was checked in and around Villers-Bocage and withdrew west. By the following morning the 22nd Armoured Brigade Group was defending the Amayé–Tracy-Bocage–Saint-Germain sector in an all-round “box” or “island” position.

The real battle on 14 June involved brigade-scale infantry, tanks and artillery. Panzer-Lehr attacked with Tiger support from schwere SS-Panzer-Abteilung 101, while Allied artillery—including US fire from the Caumont area—helped the British defense. The Queen’s Royal Hussars account describes attacks from several directions and British tanks engaging Tigers; a 3rd Royal Horse Artillery war-diary transcript places an observation post in Amayé’s church tower supporting the withdrawal.

The game preserves the broad pattern—withdrawal after Villers-Bocage, an all-round defense near Amayé, German mortar/tank pressure, and a final British escape—but invents or compresses its squad-level details. No evidence was found for Davis, Price or MacGregor as historical participants, for the specific captured German field HQ, or for a single infantryman destroying the decisive Tiger with planted explosives. The real box was eventually abandoned not because the local defense had simply failed, but because 50th (Northumbrian) Division could not break through to link up; the 22nd Armoured Brigade Group then withdrew after midnight under Operation Aniseed to straighten the front, behind a 131st Brigade base around Briquessard. The game’s “pull back to Caumont” line points in the correct general operational direction but simplifies the actual withdrawal arrangement.
