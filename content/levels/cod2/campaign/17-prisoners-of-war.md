## The Mission in the Game

“Prisoners of War” is a singleplayer mission in *Call of Duty 2*. The player is Sergeant John Davis of the British 7th Armoured Division, fighting under Captain Price and alongside MacGregor. The mission is dated **11 June 1944**. Its gameplay title card places the action at **Beltot, France**, while the pre-mission diary is datelined **Caumont, France**. The transcript describes the wider assignment as probing around the western flank of the German forces blocking Caen.

The level begins with the British infantry splitting around orchards and hedgerows to attack the settlement. Davis helps suppress and flank German positions, clears houses, and destroys mortar crews firing from a ruined building. In the final house, the unit finds two surrendering German soldiers with several wounded American soldiers. Price stops a British soldier from shooting the surrendering Germans and orders MacGregor and Davis to recover a German lorry so the wounded Americans can be evacuated.

On the return trip the pair encounters a German counterattack and a Tiger tank. Davis destroys the tank with captured Panzerschrecks from the back of the moving lorry before the truck reaches the wounded soldiers.

The transcript contains an important internal geographic inconsistency. The diary says **Caumont**, the mission itself says **Beltot**, and the title card describes Beltot as **18 miles east of Caen**. The real Beltot identified below is west-southwest of Caen, not east. These captions therefore should not be treated as a precise reconstruction of the real route.

## The Real Place & Differences

The strongest real-world match is the actual **hamlet of Beltot in Calvados, Normandy**, now within the commune of Aurseulles. Current OpenStreetMap-derived mapping identifies Beltot as a hamlet at approximately **49.12308, -0.67503**. A French toponymy entry for nearby/former Orbois also independently records the name “hameau de Beltot.” This establishes that Beltot is a genuine local place-name rather than an invented village.

The match is best classified as a **confirmed real location at settlement level**, not as a confirmed reconstruction of a specific battlefield. Beltot lies in the same western-Caen bocage belt used by the later British campaign missions: the following levels name Anctoville, St. Louet and Amayé-sur-Seulles, all real places in the same broader area. That clustering strengthens the interpretation that the campaign deliberately borrowed local Normandy toponyms.

The landscape is broadly plausible. The level uses orchards, hedges, narrow approaches, farm buildings and close-range fighting around houses. A contemporary 1/5th Queen’s war diary describes the fighting south of Tilly-sur-Seulles on 12 June as taking place among houses, hedges and ditches in “extremely close country.” That supports the general bocage character of the game environment, but it does **not** show that the designers reproduced Beltot’s real street plan, individual farmhouses, mortar position or lorry route.

The game’s “18 miles east of Caen” caption is incompatible with the mapped real Beltot, which lies to the west-southwest of Caen. The preloader’s use of Caumont is also distinct from the gameplay location. Caumont belongs to the real 7th Armoured Division’s next operational movement, but that movement began after the fighting around Tilly and is documented on 12 June, not as the unit’s 11 June position.

## The Real Mission & Differences

No documented historical **“Prisoners of War” mission at Beltot on 11 June 1944** was found in the reviewed unit record and official-history material. The closest documented 7th Armoured Division context is the fighting on the **Tilly-sur-Seulles** front during Operation Perch.

The 131st (Queen’s) Brigade was the lorried infantry brigade of the 7th Armoured Division; it included the 1/5th, 1/6th and 1/7th battalions of the Queen’s Royal Regiment. The 1/5th Queen’s war diary records that on **11 June** the battalion moved through Bayeux along the Tilly-sur-Seulles road and established positions with the other Queen’s battalions. The same diary records German prisoners from the 12th SS Panzer Division and Panzer Lehr Division and reports fighting for Tilly in which the 2nd Gloucesters were twice driven out by tanks, including some Tigers.

Those details make several ingredients of the game historically credible at a broad level—German prisoners, heavy armor, villages and close bocage fighting—but they do not document the level’s particular events. The sources reviewed do not identify Sergeant John Davis, Captain Price or MacGregor as historical participants, do not record wounded American prisoners being liberated at Beltot, and do not describe a British pair retrieving a German lorry and destroying a Tiger with captured Panzerschrecks there.

The chronology also differs materially. The 1/5th Queen’s diary records continued fighting south of Tilly on **12 June**, followed at 21:45 by a withdrawal after the corps decision to switch the 7th Armoured Division toward the American drive on **Caumont** and reach **Villers-Bocage** by that route. The U.S. Army’s *Cross-Channel Attack* likewise describes Panzer Lehr checking the 7th Armoured Division around Tilly on 10–11 June, then the new flanking move beginning on 12 June; leading British elements passed Livry that afternoon and entered Villers-Bocage the next morning.

Accordingly, the game compresses the historical sequence. Its June 11 narration already frames Davis as circling around the western flank and its diary places him at Caumont, whereas the documented 7th Armoured Division was still engaged around Tilly on June 11 and only turned toward the Caumont gap on June 12. The mission is therefore best understood as a **fictionalized/composite combat episode placed at a real Normandy hamlet**, borrowing real operational themes from the opening phase of Operation Perch without matching a documented action at Beltot.
