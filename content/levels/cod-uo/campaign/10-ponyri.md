## The Mission in the Game

“Ponyri” is the second Soviet campaign mission in *Call of Duty: United
Offensive*. The player again controls the fictional Private Yuri Petrenko,
identified by the game as a member of the 67th Guards Rifle Division. His
letter is dated 9 July 1943 and says that his formation has been redeployed
north from Cherkasskoye. The playable action begins just outside Ponyri at
11:00 on 11 July.

Sergeant Antonov orders the soldiers to advance behind T-34 tanks and capture
the railway station. Petrenko kills a marksman in a tower, clears the station
building and then fights through houses and streets. The Soviet force pushes
on to a large industrial building described as a factory or tank depot, enters
through its basement and destroys a German tank inside. Lieutenant Denisovich
then declares that the Germans have been driven from Ponyri.

The route contains several features that resemble genuine Ponyri objectives:
the railway, station building, prominent tower and industrial or maintenance
compound. The level nevertheless compresses several days of fighting into a
single continuous assault and assigns the action to the wrong Soviet
division.

## The Real Place & Differences

Ponyri is an urban-type settlement in Kursk Oblast on the railway between Oryol
and Kursk. Contemporary military accounts often call the objective “Ponyri
Station,” distinguishing it from nearby settlements such as Ponyri 1, Ponyri
2 and Pervaya Maya. The railway ran approximately north to south and made the
station a useful approach toward Kursk. Buildings around the station became
fortified strongpoints in a battle fought across fields, rail embankments,
streets and small settlements rather than through one continuous town centre.

The game’s station objective has a strong real counterpart. Historical
accounts identify the station building, railway school, water-supply point or
water tower, and machine-tractor station as repeatedly contested positions.
The game’s sniper tower may evoke the real Shukhov water tower beside the
station, but no evidence was found that its model or scripted sniper action is
a deliberate reconstruction. The wartime tower was damaged, restored shortly
after the battle and demolished several years later; only its former site can
be associated with the present station area.

The final “factory” is probably a fictional enlargement of Ponyri’s real
machine-tractor station or another railway maintenance structure. Ponyri was a
rural railway settlement, and the sources consulted do not identify a German
tank factory there. Fighting over a tractor depot and disabled armour is
historically plausible, but a functioning tank-production or major repair
plant is not established.

Ponyri was devastated during the July fighting. The current station and
settlement therefore provide a geographical anchor, not an unchanged copy of
the 1943 streetscape. The local historical museum’s central diorama depicts
the battle for the station on 10 July and preserves the event’s association
with this exact part of the settlement.

## The Real Mission & Differences

Ponyri lay on the northern face of the Kursk salient in the Soviet **Central
Front’s 13th Army** sector. When the German 9th Army redirected much of its
effort toward Ponyri, the settlement became one of the most fiercely contested
points of Operation Citadel. German attacks reached the station area on 7
July, after which buildings and railway facilities changed hands repeatedly.

The principal Soviet formation defending Ponyri Station was Major General
Mikhail Enshin’s **307th Rifle Division**, especially its 1019th and 1023rd
Rifle Regiments. It received exceptionally heavy support from artillery,
engineers, the 129th Tank Brigade, other tank units and later the 3rd and 4th
Guards Airborne Divisions. The US Army Combat Studies Institute account
describes the station changing hands during hours of close combat and records
counterattacks supported by the 51st and 129th Tank Brigades. Soviet use of
tanks alongside infantry in the game is therefore credible in general terms.

The mission’s 67th Guards Rifle Division attribution is not historically
credible. That division belonged to the 6th Guards Army on the **southern**
face of the salient and was still fighting west of the Oboyan road around
10-12 July. It could not have transferred roughly across the salient in the
short interval described by Petrenko’s letter. The letter compounds the error
by saying the unit was temporarily attached to the 6th Guards Army: the 67th
Guards already served with that army, whereas the real Ponyri battle belonged
to the 13th Army. Petrenko and his squad remain fictional devices connecting
two historically separate sectors.

The date of **11 July** falls within the accepted 5-11 July Ponyri defensive
operation, but the level more closely resembles a composite of the Soviet
counterattacks of 8-10 July. By the night of 10-11 July, the exhausted 307th
Rifle Division was being relieved by the 3rd and 4th Guards Airborne
Divisions. German attacks continued in the area on the 11th, but by that
evening the German 9th Army’s attempt to break through the 13th Army sector
had failed; on 12 July German armoured units began withdrawing and laying
defensive minefields.

The game consequently reaches the broadly correct outcome—Ponyri remained in
Soviet hands and the German northern thrust failed—but simplifies a prolonged,
seesaw defence into one victorious advance. Capturing the station, clearing
the whole settlement and storming the factory were not one documented mission
by the 67th Guards Rifle Division. They combine real battlefield landmarks,
actions by several Soviet formations and invented squad-level events.
