## The Mission in the Game

“Kharkov Station” is the final campaign mission in *Call of Duty: United
Offensive*. The player again controls the fictional Private Yuri Petrenko,
whose squad is now presented as part of the 15th Guards Rifle Division. The
mission begins in Kharkov at 16:15 on 22 August 1943, shortly after the events
of “Kharkov Outskirts” (`cod-uo-kharkov-1`).

Petrenko's letter says the force is nearing the main square and intends to
reach the railway station. The squad captures a ruined square, protects
engineers while they clear rubble for Soviet tanks, then uses a captured German
anti-aircraft gun against bombers. It flanks through an unnamed factory and
descends into the railway complex. Petrenko helps hold the tracks against
German infantry, aircraft and tanks, using another German gun against the
armoured counterattack.

The finale has a train arrive directly through the contested station carrying
Soviet infantry and armour. The reinforcements charge from the train, German
forces flee and a commissar declares Kharkov liberated. This is a cinematic
ending to both the mission and the game's campaign, not a documented sequence
of events at the station.

## The Real Place & Differences

The briefing in the preceding mission calls the objective Kharkov's **central
train station**. The strongest real match is therefore
**Kharkiv-Pasazhyrskyi**, historically also known as Kharkov-Passenger or the
Southern Station. It stands on Pryvokzalnyi Square west of central Kharkiv and
has been the city's principal passenger terminal since the nineteenth century.

The coordinate match is secure, but the architecture is not. The large station
building that existed during the war was destroyed, and the current monumental
terminal opened in 1952. A present-day photograph or building comparison
cannot be used to authenticate the game's model. The level also supplies no
recognizable street names, railway structures or sight lines that would allow
its square, factory and approach route to be overlaid on the real station
district.

The “main square” passed earlier in the level should not automatically be read
as Dzerzhinsky Square, now Freedom Square. That major civic square lies well
northeast of the passenger station, and the game gives no evidence for the
connecting route. Pryvokzalnyi Square immediately outside the terminal is a
possible visual concept, but the mission leaves the square and passes through
a factory before reaching the tracks, so even that identification remains
uncertain.

## The Real Mission & Differences

The real fighting belonged to the final phase of the Belgorod-Kharkiv
offensive. German forces began their organized withdrawal on 22 August, and
the Steppe Front ordered its armies to storm Kharkiv during the night of
22–23 August. Soviet formations entered from several directions: the 53rd and
69th Armies from the west and north, and the 7th Guards Army from the east.

The game's **15th Guards Rifle Division** attribution is historically relevant
to Kharkiv but not to the central passenger station. A compiled account from
the 69th Army states that Kharkiv railway station was taken at 03:30 on
23 August after the 89th and 93rd Guards and the 375th and 183rd Rifle
Divisions entered the city. The same source records the 15th Guards, part of
the 7th Guards Army, reaching the eastern outskirts at 04:00 and taking
**Osnova Railway Station** at 09:00 before moving farther into Kharkiv. No
evidence was found that the 15th Guards captured Kharkiv-Pasazhyrskyi.

The game also starts too early. Its 16:15 timestamp on 22 August predates the
Steppe Front's approximately 20:00 order for the night assault and places the
station battle more than eleven hours before the reported 03:30 capture.
Changing the date to the early hours of 23 August and assigning the western
station approach to formations of the 69th Army would fit the documented
chronology more closely.

The scale of resistance is dramatized. Historical reconstructions describe
most German troops already withdrawing, with covering groups, ruined streets,
destroyed bridges and abandoned equipment slowing the Soviet advance. Local
fighting did occur, but the mission's prolonged combined counterattack by
tanks, bombers and infantry at the station is not documented. The individual
engineer losses, captured anti-aircraft guns and Petrenko's defence of the
tracks are fictional set pieces.

The reinforcement train is the clearest material difference. Railway records
describe engineers beginning urgent restoration work in the surviving portion
of the terminal on 26 August. The first post-liberation train reached Kharkiv
on 29 August, and through military traffic and other station connections were
restored only afterward. A train carrying battle-ready infantry and tanks into
Kharkiv-Pasazhyrskyi while combat still raged on 22 August is therefore not
historically credible.

The ending also compresses the outcome. Central Kharkiv was occupied by Soviet
forces on 23 August, the date used in the official victory order, but fighting
continued on southern and western outskirts for several more days. The
commissar's declaration turns a multi-army operation, organized German
withdrawal and continuing peripheral combat into one immediate victory caused
by the station's defence.
